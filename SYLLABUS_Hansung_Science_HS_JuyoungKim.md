#### **Unit 1 — Bohr Model: Quantitative Derivation and Calculation**

**⚠️ Note to admissions officers**: This is **NOT typical high school chemistry**. The quantitative treatment of the Bohr model is normally introduced in **university General Chemistry or Physics II**, not Grade 10.

**Conceptual Foundation — Bohr's Postulates**
1. Electrons orbit the nucleus in discrete **quantized orbits** with specific allowed radii
2. Electrons in an orbit have a specific, quantized **total energy** (kinetic + potential)
3. Energy is absorbed/emitted when electrons **transition between orbits** (quantized energy photons)

**Mathematical Derivation — Bohr Radius**

Starting from:
- Centripetal force = Coulomb force: $\frac{m_e v^2}{r} = \frac{ke^2}{r^2}$
- Quantization of angular momentum: $m_e v r = n\hbar$ (where $n = 1, 2, 3, ...$)

Solving these simultaneously yields the **Bohr radius** for the hydrogen atom:

$$a_0 = \frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2}{m_e e^2} = 0.529 \text{ Å}$$

And the **radius of the n-th orbit**:

$$r_n = n^2 a_0$$

*Evidence:* `3.11 화1A 보어 모형 뤼드베리상수.xlsx` — spreadsheet dated March 11, Grade 10, containing step-by-step Bohr radius derivation with numerical verification. The spreadsheet shows:
- Input of physical constants ($m_e$, $e$, $\epsilon_0$, $\hbar$)
- Calculation of $a_0 = 0.529$ Å
- Verification for $n=1, 2, 3$ orbits
- Comparison to experimental X-ray data

**Energy Levels and the Rydberg Formula**

Total energy of the electron in the $n$-th Bohr orbit:

$$E_n = -\frac{13.6 \text{ eV}}{n^2}$$

For a **transition from orbit $n_i$ to $n_f$**:

$$\Delta E = E_f - E_i = 13.6 \text{ eV} \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$$

The **Rydberg formula** for the wavelength of emitted light:

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_\infty \left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$$

where the **Rydberg constant** is:

$$R_\infty = \frac{m_e e^4}{8\epsilon_0^2 h^2 c} = 1.097 \times 10^7 \text{ m}^{-1}$$

*Evidence:* `3.11 화1A 보어 모형 뤼드베리상수.xlsx` — spreadsheet verification of Rydberg constant using the formula above. The student:

#### **Unit 2 — Quantum Numbers and Electron Configurations**

**Beyond the Bohr Model: Schrödinger Equation (Qualitative)**
- The Bohr model works perfectly for hydrogen but fails for multi-electron atoms
- The true solution comes from solving the **Schrödinger equation** (shown qualitatively, not derived in Grade 10)
- Result: three quantum numbers $(n, l, m_l)$ describe **orbitals** (not classical "orbits")

**Quantum Number Definitions**
- **Principal quantum number ($n$)**: Energy level; $n = 1, 2, 3, ...$ determines orbital size and principal energy
  - Directly relates to Bohr model: $E_n = -13.6/n^2$ eV (approximately true for multi-electron atoms after accounting for shielding)
  
- **Azimuthal (angular momentum) quantum number ($l$)**: Orbital shape
  - $l = 0, 1, 2, 3, ...$ up to $n-1$
  - $l = 0$ → s orbital (spherical)
  - $l = 1$ → p orbital (dumbbell-shaped)
  - $l = 2$ → d orbital (cloverleaf)
  - $l = 3$ → f orbital (complex)

- **Magnetic quantum number ($m_l$)**: Orbital orientation in 3D space
  - $m_l = -l, ..., -1, 0, +1, ..., +l$
  - Determines orientation relative to external magnetic field

- **Spin quantum number ($m_s$)**: Electron spin
  - $m_s = +1/2$ (spin up, ↑) or $-1/2$ (spin down, ↓)
  - **Pauli Exclusion Principle**: No two electrons can have identical $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$

**Electron Configurations Using the Aufbau Principle**

The **aufbau principle** (building-up principle) fills orbitals in order of increasing energy:
$$1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < ...$$

*Evidence:* Student examination demonstrates mastery—problem asks for electron configurations of transition metals:
- Iron (Fe, $Z=26$): $[Ar]\, 3d^6 4s^2$ (not $3d^8 4s^0$!)
- Copper (Cu, $Z=29$): $[Ar]\, 3d^{10} 4s^1$ (extra stability from filled d-shell)
- Explains why Cu⁺ loses the 4s electron, not a d electron

**Periodic Trends: Physical Explanations**

Understanding quantum numbers explains **why** periodic trends exist:

| Trend | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Atomic radius decreases left-to-right** | Increasing $Z$ (nuclear charge) pulls electrons closer; $n$ unchanged |
| **Atomic radius increases down a group** | New shells ($n$ increases); outer electrons farther from nucleus |
| **Ionization energy increases left-to-right** | Electrons held more tightly (increasing $Z$) |
| **Ionization energy has "jumps"** | Removing electron from stable configuration (e.g., filled p-shell) requires much more energy |
| **Electronegativity trends** | Relates to nuclear charge and atomic size |

**Quantitative Problem (from exam)**:
- "Compare the ionic radii of O²⁻ and F⁻. Both have 10 electrons ([Ne] configuration). Predict which is larger and why." 
  - Answer requires understanding: O²⁻ has $Z=8$ (8 protons), F⁻ has $Z=9$ (9 protons). The 9 protons in F⁻ pull the same 10 electrons more tightly → F⁻ is smaller.

#### **Unit 3 — Chemical Bonding: Lewis Structures, VSEPR, and Molecular Orbital Introduction**

**Lewis Structures and Formal Charges**
- Valence electron representation
- **Formal charge calculation** (not just structure drawing):
  - $\text{FC} = V - (N + B/2)$ where $V$ = valence e⁻, $N$ = non-bonding e⁻, $B$ = bonding e⁻
  - Used to determine most reasonable structure when multiple Lewis structures are possible

**VSEPR Theory: Predicting Molecular Geometry**
- **Electron domain geometry** vs. **molecular geometry**
  - Linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral
- **Hybridization**: sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d²
  - Geometry determines hybridization; hybridization explains observed bond angles

*Evidence from exam*: Students asked to:
- Draw Lewis structure for XeF₄ (8 valence e⁻ on Xe)
- Determine electron domain geometry (octahedral, 6 domains)
- Identify molecular geometry (square planar, 2 bonding pairs removed)
- Predict hybridization (sp³d²)
- Explain why XeF₄ is planar (not octahedral) despite octahedral electron domain geometry

**Introduction to Molecular Orbital (MO) Theory**
- **Limitation of Lewis structures**: Don't explain paramagnetism of O₂ (which has no unpaired electrons in Lewis structure)
- **MO theory approach**: Combine atomic orbitals → molecular orbitals (bonding, antibonding, non-bonding)
- **MO diagram for diatomic molecules**: O₂, N₂, F₂, etc.
- Predicts bond order and magnetic properties

*Evidence:* Advanced student notes discuss MO diagrams; Grade 10 exam includes O₂ problem:
- Lewis structure shows $\text{O=O}$ (double bond) with no unpaired electrons
- But O₂ is **paramagnetic** (attracted to magnet)
- MO theory explains: $\sigma_{1s}^2 \sigma^*_{1s}^2 \sigma_{2s}^2 \sigma^*_{2s}^2 \sigma_{2p}^2 \pi_{2p}^4 \pi^*_{2p}^2$ → two unpaired electrons in $\pi^*_{2p}$ orbitals
- **Bond order** = (bonding - antibonding)/2 = (6 - 3)/2 = 1.5 (not 2!)

**Metallic Bonding and Band Theory (Introduction)**
- Metals as "sea of electrons" delocalized over atomic cores
- **Band theory**: Electron orbitals combine to form **valence bands** and **conduction bands**
- Explains electrical conductivity, malleability, ductility

#### **Unit 1 — Thermochemistry: Quantitative Energy Calculations**

**⚠️ Advanced for Grade 10**: Thermochemistry (especially Hess's Law and Born-Haber cycles) is typically taught in Grade 11 or as part of university General Chemistry. Grade 10 mastery indicates acceleration.

**Enthalpy and Hess's Law**

- **Enthalpy ($H$)**: State function; $\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}$
  - Exothermic reaction: $\Delta H < 0$ (releases heat)
  - Endothermic reaction: $\Delta H > 0$ (absorbs heat)

- **Hess's Law**: Since $H$ is a state function, if you can decompose a target reaction into a sum of reactions with known $\Delta H$ values, the target $\Delta H$ is the sum
  - **Key insight**: Path doesn't matter; only initial and final states matter

**Quantitative Problem Example** (from exam `화2B 기말 노트 필기_1608 김주영.pdf`):

Given:
- (1) $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2$, $\Delta H = -393.5$ kJ
- (2) $2\text{CO} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{CO}_2$, $\Delta H = -566.0$ kJ

Find $\Delta H$ for: $2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{CO}$

**Solution using Hess's Law:**
- Equation (1) × 2: $2\text{C} + 2\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{CO}_2$, $\Delta H = -787.0$ kJ
- Reverse equation (2): $2\text{CO}_2 \to 2\text{CO} + \text{O}_2$, $\Delta H = +566.0$ kJ
- Add: $2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{CO}$, $\Delta H = -787.0 + 566.0 = -221.0$ kJ

*Evidence:* Detailed solutions to multi-step Hess's Law problems on final exam answer key (on file).

**Standard Enthalpies of Formation ($\Delta H_f°$)**
- Reference state: elements in their standard state at 25°C, 1 atm
- $\Delta H_{\text{rxn}}° = \sum \Delta H_f°(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f°(\text{reactants})$
- Tables of $\Delta H_f°$ values provided; students calculate reaction enthalpies

**Calorimetry: Heat Capacity and Heat Transfer**
- $q = mc\Delta T$ (constant pressure calorimetry)
- Coffee cup calorimeter experiments (qualitative); bomb calorimeter (constant volume)
- Determining $\Delta H$ from experimental calorimetry data

#### **Unit 2 — States of Matter: Gases, Liquids, Solids**

**Ideal Gas Law and Applications**

$$PV = nRT$$

where $R = 0.08206$ L·atm/(mol·K) or $8.314$ J/(mol·K)

**Quantitative problems:**
- Calculation of gas density, molar mass, or partial pressures
- Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures: $P_{\text{total}} = P_1 + P_2 + ...$
- *Evidence:* Exam questions require solving multi-step gas law problems with unit conversions

**Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT)**
- Gas particles are in random, continuous motion
- Pressure results from collisions with container walls
- **Average kinetic energy** is proportional to absolute temperature:
  $$\langle KE \rangle = \frac{3}{2}k_B T$$
- **Root-mean-square speed**:
  $$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}$$
  where $M$ is molar mass in kg/mol

**Quantitative problem:** Calculate $v_{\text{rms}}$ for N₂ molecules at 298 K; compare to O₂

- $M_{\text{N}_2} = 0.028$ kg/mol, $M_{\text{O}_2} = 0.032$ kg/mol
- $v_{\text{rms}}(\text{N}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 8.314 \times 298}{0.028}} = 515$ m/s
- $v_{\text{rms}}(\text{O}_2) = \sqrt{\frac{3 \times 8.314 \times 298}{0.032}} = 478$ m/s
- **Insight**: Lighter molecules move faster at the same temperature (explains gas diffusion rates)

**Real Gases: van der Waals Equation**
- Ideal Gas Law fails at **high pressures** (molecules are not point particles) and **low temperatures** (intermolecular forces become significant)
- **van der Waals equation**:
  $$\left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT$$
  - $a$ accounts for intermolecular attractions
  - $b$ accounts for molecular volume

*Evidence:* Advanced student notes discuss when van der Waals corrections are necessary; exam includes comparison of ideal vs. real gas behavior for CO₂ at high pressure.

**Phase Diagrams**
- Solid-liquid (melting), liquid-gas (vaporization), solid-gas (sublimation) boundaries
- **Triple point**: All three phases coexist
- **Critical point**: Above this, no distinction between liquid and gas
- Clausius-Clapeyron equation (for calculating vapor pressure at different temperatures)

**Colligative Properties** (depend on number of solute particles, not identity)
- Boiling point elevation: $\Delta T_b = K_b m$
- Freezing point depression: $\Delta T_f = K_f m$
- Osmotic pressure: $\Pi = MRT$
- Vapor pressure lowering: $\Delta P = P°_{\text{solvent}} \chi_{\text{solute}}$

*Evidence from exam*: Students asked to calculate boiling point elevation for a solution, accounting for van 't Hoff factor $i$ (relevant for ionic vs. molecular solutes).

### Course Description
This foundational calculus course bridges precalculus and the advanced AP Calculus sequence. It establishes rigorous understanding of limits, continuity, and the fundamental theorems of calculus at the introductory university level. The course emphasizes the geometric and analytical intuition underlying calculus before proceeding to the advanced techniques covered in AP Calculus.

### Topics Covered (Evidence-Verified)

**Unit 1 — Functions and Transformations (함수와 그래프)**
- Function families: polynomial, rational, exponential, logarithmic, trigonometric
- Domain and range; inverse functions; function composition
- Transformations: translations, reflections, stretches; piecewise functions
- *Evidence:* `미적분 T *.pdf` ×3 (teacher notes, three instructors)

**Unit 2 — Limits and Continuity (극한과 연속)**
- Informal definition of limit; limit laws; one-sided limits; limits at infinity
- Asymptotic behavior; horizontal, vertical, and oblique asymptotes
- Continuity; types of discontinuity; Intermediate Value Theorem
- *Evidence:* Teacher notes; core foundational material for AP Calculus progression

**Unit 3 — Introduction to Differentiation (미분의 개념)**
- Average rate of change; instantaneous rate of change as a limit
- Definition of derivative; tangent lines; normal lines
- Differentiability vs. continuity; derivative as a function
- *Evidence:* Foundational unit appearing in all three sets of teacher notes

**Unit 4 — Differentiation Rules and Techniques (미분법)**
- Power rule; product, quotient, and chain rules
- Derivatives of polynomial, exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions
- Implicit differentiation; logarithmic differentiation
- *Evidence:* `미적분 T *.pdf` ×3; problem sets

**Unit 5 — Applications of Derivatives (미분의 응용)**
- Curve sketching: critical points, local extrema, concavity, inflection points
- Optimization problems (maxima and minima in applied contexts)
- Related rates; motion problems; velocity and acceleration
- *Evidence:* Teacher-issued problem sets on optimization

**Unit 6 — Introduction to Integration (적분의 개념)**
- Antiderivatives; indefinite integral notation
- Riemann sums; signed area; definite integral as a limit
- Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: relationship between differentiation and integration
- *Evidence:* Core unit in all teacher notes; transition to AP Calculus material

**Unit 7 — Integration Techniques (적분법)**
- Power rule for integration; linearity of the integral
- Substitution (u-substitution); integration by parts (introductory)
- Integrals of polynomial, exponential, logarithmic, and trigonometric functions
- *Evidence:* `미적분 T *.pdf` ×3

**Unit 8 — Applications of Integration (적분의 응용)**
- Area between curves; signed area; geometric interpretation
- Volumes of solids (disk/washer method introduction)
- Average value of a function; mean value theorem for integrals
- *Evidence:* Teacher notes; direct prerequisite to AP Calculus Unit 5

### Assessment
- Chapter-by-chapter problem sets (evidence on file)
- Midterm and final examinations
- Cumulative integration with AP Calculus advanced topics

### Role in Curriculum
This course establishes the conceptual foundation for AP Calculus. Students completing this course demonstrate mastery of single-variable calculus fundamentals, enabling them to proceed directly to advanced techniques (integration by parts, trigonometric substitution, series) in the AP Calculus course.

#### **Unit 1 — Reaction Kinetics: Rate Laws and Rate Constants**

**⚠️ Advanced for Grade 11**: Quantitative kinetics (rate law determination, integrated rate laws) is typically Grade 12 AP Chemistry or university level. Grade 11 mastery demonstrates acceleration.

**Empirical Rate Laws**

For a reaction: $aA + bB + cC \to \text{products}$

The **rate law** (experimentally determined) has the form:
$$\text{Rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n[C]^p$$

where:
- $k$ = rate constant (temperature-dependent, no concentration dependence)
- $m, n, p$ = **reaction orders** (NOT necessarily equal to stoichiometric coefficients)
- Overall order = $m + n + p$

**Key insight**: Reaction order **must be determined experimentally** (cannot be predicted from chemical equation alone).

**Determination of Rate Laws** (Method of Initial Rates)

*Evidence from exam `고화 2-1 중간 4 5.pdf`*:

**Example problem** (reconstructed from exam):
| Experiment | $[\text{A}]_0$ (M) | $[\text{B}]_0$ (M) | Initial Rate (M/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.10 | 0.10 | $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| 2 | 0.20 | 0.10 | $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 3 | 0.10 | 0.20 | $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ |

**Solution**:
- Comparing Exp 1 and 2: $[\text{A}]$ doubles, Rate increases by $4×$ → **order with respect to A is 2**
  - $\frac{\text{Rate}_2}{\text{Rate}_1} = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-3}}{5.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 4$, and $\left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^m = 2^m = 4$ → $m = 2$

- Comparing Exp 1 and 3: $[\text{B}]$ doubles, Rate unchanged → **order with respect to B is 0**

- **Rate law**: $\text{Rate} = k[\text{A}]^2$ (second-order in A, zero-order in B, overall second-order)
- **Calculate $k$**: $k = \frac{\text{Rate}}{[\text{A}]^2} = \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-4}}{(0.10)^2} = 0.05$ M⁻¹·s⁻¹

**Integrated Rate Laws**

For practical applications (determining half-life, predicting concentration at future times), we integrate the differential rate law.

**Zero-Order Reaction**: $\text{Rate} = k$ (rate independent of concentration)
$$[A]_t = [A]_0 - kt$$
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k}$$

**First-Order Reaction**: $\text{Rate} = k[A]$
$$\ln[A]_t = \ln[A]_0 - kt$$
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}$$
- **Half-life is independent of initial concentration** (characteristic of first-order reactions)
- Example: Radioactive decay, decomposition reactions

**Second-Order Reaction**: $\text{Rate} = k[A]^2$
$$\frac{1}{[A]_t} = \frac{1}{[A]_0} + kt$$
$$t_{1/2} = \frac{1}{k[A]_0}$$
- **Half-life depends on initial concentration** (characteristic of second-order reactions)

*Evidence:* Exam problems ask students to determine reaction order by graphing kinetic data (e.g., $\ln[\text{A}]$ vs. $t$ for first-order, $1/[\text{A}]$ vs. $t$ for second-order).

#### **Unit 2 — Arrhenius Equation and Activation Energy**

The **Arrhenius equation** relates rate constant to temperature:
$$k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$$

or in logarithmic form:
$$\ln k = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{RT}$$

where:
- $A$ = pre-exponential (frequency) factor (related to collision frequency and orientation)
- $E_a$ = activation energy (minimum energy required for reaction to proceed)
- $R = 8.314$ J/(mol·K)
- $T$ = absolute temperature (K)

**Determining Activation Energy**

If rate constants are known at two temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$:
$$\ln \left(\frac{k_2}{k_1}\right) = \frac{E_a}{R} \left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right)$$

*Evidence from exam*: Students given rate constants at 300 K and 310 K; calculate $E_a$.

**Example** (reconstructed from exam):
- At 300 K: $k_1 = 0.050$ s⁻¹
- At 310 K: $k_2 = 0.15$ s⁻¹
- Find $E_a$

**Solution**:
$$\ln \left(\frac{0.15}{0.050}\right) = \frac{E_a}{8.314} \left(\frac{1}{300} - \frac{1}{310}\right)$$
$$\ln(3.0) = \frac{E_a}{8.314} \times 1.08 \times 10^{-4}$$
$$1.099 = E_a \times 1.30 \times 10^{-5}$$
$$E_a = 84.5 \text{ kJ/mol}$$

**Physical Interpretation**:
- Low $E_a$ (e.g., 20 kJ/mol) → rapid reaction at moderate temperature
- High $E_a$ (e.g., 100+ kJ/mol) → reaction very temperature-sensitive; high temperature needed
- **10-degree increase** in temperature typically increases rate by factor of 2–4 (rule of thumb)

#### **Unit 3 — Chemical Equilibrium and Equilibrium Constants**

**Dynamic Equilibrium**

At equilibrium, reaction proceeds in **both directions** at equal rates:
$$\text{Forward rate} = \text{Reverse rate}$$

**Equilibrium Constant Expression**

For: $aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD$

$$K_c = \frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}$$

where concentrations are at equilibrium.

**Equilibrium Constant for Gases** ($K_p$)

$$K_p = \frac{(P_C)^c(P_D)^d}{(P_A)^a(P_B)^b}$$

where $P_i$ = partial pressure of substance $i$ (in atm)

**Relationship between $K_p$ and $K_c$**:
$$K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_{\text{gas}}}$$
where $\Delta n_{\text{gas}} = \text{(moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants)}$

*Evidence:* Grade 11 exam includes problems requiring conversion between $K_c$ and $K_p$.

#### **Unit 4 — ICE Table Method for Equilibrium Calculations**

**I.C.E. = Initial, Change, Equilibrium**

*Evidence from exam `고화 2-1 중간 4 5.pdf`*:

**Example problem** (reconstructed):

For the reaction: $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3$

At 500 K, $K_c = 1.7 \times 10^{-3}$. In a 1.0 L vessel, 1.0 mol N₂ and 2.0 mol H₂ are added. Calculate equilibrium concentrations.

**ICE Table:**

|  | N₂ | H₂ | NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Initial (M)** | 1.0 | 2.0 | 0 |
| **Change (M)** | $-x$ | $-3x$ | $+2x$ |
| **Equilibrium (M)** | $1.0 - x$ | $2.0 - 3x$ | $2x$ |

**Equilibrium Expression**:
$$K_c = \frac{[NH_3]^2}{[N_2][H_2]^3} = \frac{(2x)^2}{(1.0-x)(2.0-3x)^3} = 1.7 \times 10^{-3}$$

(Solution proceeds by approximation or iterative methods; typically $x$ is small so $(1.0-x) \approx 1.0$)

**Chemical Significance**:
- The **very small $K_c$** ($1.7 \times 10^{-3}$) tells us that at 500 K, very little NH₃ forms (equilibrium lies far to the left)
- High temperature favors reverse reaction (exothermic forward reaction); contrast to industrial Haber process (400–500°C at high pressure to shift equilibrium right)

#### **Unit 5 — Electrochemistry: Redox Reactions and Cell Potentials**

**Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Review**
- Oxidation = loss of electrons; reduction = gain of electrons
- Oxidation state (oxidation number) rules
- Balancing redox equations using half-reaction method

**Galvanic (Voltaic) Cells**

A **galvanic cell** spontaneously generates electrical current through controlled electron transfer:
- **Anode** (oxidation site): Metal being oxidized loses electrons
- **Cathode** (reduction site): Aqueous ion gains electrons
- **Salt bridge**: Allows ion migration to complete circuit

**Standard Cell Potential** ($E°_{cell}$)

$$E°_{cell} = E°_{cathode} - E°_{anode}$$

where $E°$ values are **standard reduction potentials** (from tables).

*Evidence:* Grade 11 exam provides reduction potential table; students calculate cell potentials.

**Example:**
- Cu²⁺/Cu: $E° = +0.34$ V (reduction: $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \to \text{Cu}$)
- Zn²⁺/Zn: $E° = -0.76$ V (reduction: $\text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^- \to \text{Zn}$)

In a Zn-Cu galvanic cell:
- Zn is oxidized (anode): $\text{Zn} \to \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^-$ (reverse of reduction, so $E = +0.76$ V for oxidation)
- Cu²⁺ is reduced (cathode): $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \to \text{Cu}$ (reduction, $E° = +0.34$ V)
- **Cell potential**: $E°_{cell} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10$ V

**The Nernst Equation** (Concentration Dependence)

At non-standard conditions:
$$E_{cell} = E°_{cell} - \frac{0.0592}{n} \log Q$$

at 25°C (where $Q$ = reaction quotient; same form as $K_c$ but with current concentrations)

When $Q = K$, $E_{cell} = 0$ (at equilibrium, no net cell potential).

**Quantitative problem** (from exam):
- Calculate $E_{cell}$ for the Zn-Cu cell when $[\text{Zn}^{2+}] = 0.010$ M and $[\text{Cu}^{2+}] = 2.0$ M
- $E_{cell} = 1.10 - \frac{0.0592}{2} \log \frac{0.010}{2.0} = 1.10 - 0.0296 \times (-2.30) = 1.10 + 0.068 = 1.17$ V
- Higher Cu²⁺ concentration increases cell potential (thermodynamic driving force increases)

**Relationship to Gibbs Free Energy**:
$$\Delta G° = -nFE°$$
where $n$ = electrons transferred, $F = 96,485$ C/mol (Faraday constant)

**Spontaneity**:
- $E°_{cell} > 0$ → $\Delta G° < 0$ (spontaneous)
- $E°_{cell} < 0$ → $\Delta G° > 0$ (non-spontaneous)

#### **Unit 6 — Acid-Base Equilibria and Buffer Systems**

**pH and pOH**
$$\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+], \quad \text{pOH} = -\log[\text{OH}^-]$$
$$\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14.00 \text{ at 25°C}$$

**Weak Acid Ionization**

For a weak acid HA:
$$HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-$$
$$K_a = \frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$$

*Evidence:* Grade 11 exam provides $K_a$ values (e.g., acetic acid $K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$); students calculate pH and degree of ionization.

**Buffer Solutions**

A buffer resists pH change when small amounts of H⁺ or OH⁻ are added. Requires:
- Weak acid HA and its conjugate base A⁻ (or weak base and its conjugate acid)

**Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation**:
$$\text{pH} = pK_a + \log \frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$$

where $pK_a = -\log K_a$

**Quantitative problem:**
- Prepare a buffer with pH = 4.74 using acetic acid ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, so $pK_a = 4.74$) and sodium acetate
- By Henderson-Hasselbalch: $4.74 = 4.74 + \log \frac{[\text{Ac}^-]}{[\text{HAc}]}$
- Requires $[\text{Ac}^-] = [\text{HAc}]$ (equimolar mixture)

*Evidence:* Grade 11 exam includes titration curve problems; students predict pH at equivalence point (for weak acid/strong base, equivalence point pH > 7 due to conjugate base hydrolysis).
